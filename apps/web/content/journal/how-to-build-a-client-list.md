I never sat down and built a client list. I still do not keep a CRM, and I should.

What I have came from four places: people I already knew, one agency house, people who found me online, and people I walked up to and asked.

If you are a restaurant chef waiting for the perfect posting to appear on a job board, I want to take that hope away from you, gently. It is not coming. The list gets built by you, one name at a time, mostly through slightly uncomfortable conversations.

## Start with friends and family

This is the easiest entry point into private cheffing and almost nobody uses it.

Tell the people you already know that you are offering the service. Private events, dinners, celebrations. Ask directly whether anything is coming up that you could cook for — a birthday, an anniversary, a holiday dinner.

Then do the first one at cost. All groceries paid for, labor free.

I know exactly how that sounds. Let me explain why I still recommend it.

That first dinner is not a job, it is an entry point, and it does four things at once. It gets you into the actual work. It starts your referral network moving. It gives you your first real credential that is not a restaurant. And — this is the one that matters most — it tells you honestly whether you want this life, or whether you just liked the idea of it. Some chefs cook one private dinner and realize they hate being alone in a stranger's kitchen. Better to learn that on a free Saturday than after you quit your job.

Then document it. Photograph the food properly, write down what you made, and post it with faces blocked. That is how you gather credibility, and credibility is how the second client shows up.

After that dinner, you charge. Do not let a generous start turn into a habit of working for free.

## The agency house is one name — a real one

My first full-time household came through Indeed and an agency. One client. Still, to this day, Monday through Wednesday I am in a house like that — households of 4 to 7 people, high net worth.

Agencies will match you with families, and they take somewhere around 15 to 40 percent of your first year. Ask that number before you sign.

After a full-time placement, I did not need them again. Every other name on my list came from me.

So think of the agency as exactly what it is: a fast way to get one very good client and learn the job properly with someone experienced beside you. It is not a client acquisition strategy. It is a starting position.

## Go where your clients already are

This is the advice I would most want a chef to actually act on, because it is the difference between hoping and hunting.

Get specific about your ideal client. Mine is affluent. They live in affluent communities. And affluent people are not hiding — they cluster in predictable places.

High-end gyms. Sports car dealerships. Private jet companies. Luxury real estate agents.

Get in contact with those businesses, and here is the angle that works: promote your service as a **gift they can give their clients.** A luxury realtor who just closed a house wants to do something memorable for the buyer. A private jet company wants to add something to the experience. You are not cold-pitching a wealthy stranger at that point — you are giving a business owner an easy way to look generous, and they hand you a warm introduction.

Ads work too, for getting your name out. Instagram DMs have booked real dinner parties for me. And again, cold messages land when you use the person's name and spend a couple of minutes on their page first.

And then there is the one I keep coming back to. I was driving through an affluent community. I stopped. I asked someone whether they needed a private chef. I was cooking for them a couple of days later.

That name is on my list because I asked. That is the entire mechanism.

## What changes after the first house

About a year in, something shifted. People started asking me to cater. People started asking for private dinners. I was no longer chasing every single name.

That is when a list starts compounding, and it comes from one thing: one good event throws off a pile of referrals.

Which is why you keep every client satisfied, even the small ones, even the ones who feel like a favor. You do not know who they are talking to at dinner on Saturday. And a bad night talks too — faster, and to more people.

Two rules I hold to on this. If I cannot take a date, I send it to someone on my team. If I have nobody available, I ask the client to rebook rather than leaving them stranded. A client list dies when you no-show, or when you say yes to something you cannot actually handle and then go quiet.

## Keeping the list alive

Here is my honest weak spot: I do not actively stay in touch, and it is a hole in my business.

Recurring clients come back so much more easily when something reminds them you exist. What I want to build is simple — a birthday note that mentions they had a good night last time and asks whether they would like another one. That is all it needs to be. Somebody reading this will build it before I do, and they will make money from it.

Be in the rooms too. Facebook groups. Reddit threads. The Brigade community. Other chefs and clients are already sitting in those rooms having conversations, and you cannot get referred from a room you are not in.

## Protect the list

Blur the faces. Cover the addresses. Get permission before a face goes up.

Your client list is private, and that is not a technicality — it is the product. If you post the house, you do not get invited back, and you also do not get the cousin's wedding, or the neighbour's anniversary dinner, or any of the referrals that would have come from a family that trusted you.

Discretion is not a restriction on your marketing. It is the reason the referrals keep coming.

## The short version

Restaurant chefs transitioning into private chef work tend to wait for a perfect opening that never posts.

Text your aunt. Cook the birthday dinner. Write the name down. Ask the next person.

That is a client list.
