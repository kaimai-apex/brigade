When I was transitioning out of restaurants, I felt like an imposter.

I did not think I had the skills yet. I could cook — I had done the sixteen-hour days, the mussels, the chervil, the fine dining discipline. But standing at the edge of private cheffing, I was convinced there was some other qualification I was missing.

What I did not understand was that the job is so much more than cooking, and that the extra part is not a qualification at all. Clients care about hospitality. How you talk to them. How you handle a situation when it goes wrong. Almost none of that is taught in a restaurant, so of course I did not have it. Neither does anybody else on their first day.

So if you are a restaurant chef reading this and thinking you are not ready — reevaluate. Count what you actually know. It is probably enough to start. The gap is the people part, and the people part is learnable in a way that fifteen years of technique is not.

## The first day is not about food

My first day as a private chef was eye-opening, and almost none of what surprised me was cooking.

It was the holistic hospitality side. Greeting the guest. Doing the shopping. Managing my own time for prep, managing my own time for cooking, with nobody calling the clock.

In a restaurant, someone else owns the schedule and someone else owns the front door. In a house, you own both, and nobody hands you the keys with an explanation.

I had someone coaching me up side by side, and I am genuinely thankful for it. It took me about two months of doing it daily before I felt like I actually knew what I was doing, and I am certain it would have taken far longer alone. If you can start with a mentor, start with a mentor.

Here is what I would emphasize above everything else: being personable is what decided my hire, more than my cooking history. Clients want to know whether you vibe with the family. Your resume gets you the meeting. The hello is what keeps you in the room.

## Where to stand

People will tell you a private chef should be invisible. I do not think that is right.

You do not need to be invisible. You need a high enough EQ to understand where you should be, at what time.

If I can hear that a serious conversation is happening between family members, I am not standing next to them. I find something at the other end of the kitchen and I stay there.

But if guests are over, I am not hiding either. I am talking to the guests, making them feel at home, being hospitable. Disappearing in that moment would be a failure, not discretion.

That is the whole skill. Knowing where you should be and where you should not be, minute to minute. In and out.

Discretion is a social skill too, not just a legal one. When a salesperson shows up at the door, I answer it and act like I am the owner of the house — because my job in that moment is to protect my client's identity. Same reflex online: you do not post their faces, you do not post their kids, you ask before any face goes up, and you cover addresses. Every one of those is a small social decision made in their favor.

## The tea

If your client drinks tea after lunch, have the water already boiled so they never wait for it.

That is it. That is the whole example, and I keep coming back to it because it captures the difference between the two jobs.

Anything your client does not have to think about is something you are quietly carrying for them, and those things accumulate into a feeling that you are indispensable. But restaurants never scheduled you to notice a tea habit. There was a runner for that, or nobody noticed and nobody minded.

In a house, there is no runner. You are the only person in the room who can see it. Which means noticing is now part of your job description.

## When the night goes bad

Four extra people show up an hour before dinner.

You can cook for them. That part is fine, you are a chef, you will solve the protein. But dinner is now moving another hour and the grocery number has changed, and the social skill here is saying that out loud — kindly, immediately, without making it awkward. Extra people come with extra cost.

Silence is how you end up absorbing it, and worse, silence is how you end up blamed for a late dinner that was never your fault.

## The worst night I have had

I cooked a bag of pasta for my client's kids. The kids are allergic to egg. There was egg in the pasta.

It was a random weeknight. I was making food for the kids and I carelessly skimmed over the ingredients list instead of reading it. I served it. Shortly after, they did not feel well.

The clients and I had a conversation about what happened, and I told them the truth — that I had not looked carefully enough at the ingredients. I thought my job was gone. I fully expected it to be.

They gave me a second chance, and I am grateful for that in a way that is hard to put into words.

In that conversation I told them how it would never happen again, what I had learned, and exactly what I would do differently going forward. Not excuses. A plan.

What it taught me is bigger than reading labels. Every mess-up is yours in this job. There is no team to disappear into. There is no big restaurant name standing behind you to absorb the blame. You take accountability, immediately and completely, because you are the only adult in that kitchen who touched the food.

That is why attention to detail is a social skill here, not just a technical one. The care you take with an ingredients list *is* the care you are taking with their family.

## How you lose the room

I have seen chefs let go for misconduct. Treating the staff and the servers unprofessionally. Partying late. Showing up late. A bad attitude that everyone in the house can feel even when nobody names it.

Word of mouth in this industry is fast, and the private world is small.

Nannies, house managers, personal assistants — you are all one operation. If you treat them like they are beneath you, the family hears about it, one way or another. Sometimes directly. More often it just becomes a feeling in the house that the chef is difficult, and that feeling is very hard to argue with.

So every single time I meet a client, I put on my best self. Walk in. Smile. Shake hands. Greet everyone in the room, not only the person paying me. Go above and beyond in a way they can actually notice.

## What I would tell you

I want to be careful not to over-glamorize this job, because I think that does real damage. My life is better now — genuinely, and by a wide margin. But there are still nights I miss family events. There are still long hours. That is the reality of a service industry, and anybody who tells you private cheffing removes all of it is selling you something.

What it does remove is the part that was breaking us: the pay, the total absence of autonomy, the sense that your passion is being used against you.

Restaurant chefs transitioning into private chef work practice their proteins constantly. Practice the door too. Practice noticing the tea. Practice the apology for the night you get it wrong, because there will be one.

That is the job they never put on the ticket, and it is the job that keeps you employed.
