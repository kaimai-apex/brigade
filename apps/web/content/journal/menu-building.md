The reason I became a chef at all was a Netflix show.

I was a year into a nursing degree at Western, which I had chosen because cooking never registered to me as something you could build a future on. Then one night I watched *The Final Table*, and I saw chefs combining flavors in ways I did not know were allowed. Specifically, burnt flavors. Burnt is bitter. Burnt is distasteful. Burnt does not belong in a dish. And there they were, folding it into something delicious on purpose.

That is what made me drop out and go to culinary school. The idea that there was that much left to learn.

I am telling you this because of what happened years later, when I finally became a private chef and discovered that I would be making chicken fingers and fries.

## The belief I had to give up

Before I started, I genuinely thought private cheffing meant I would cook the food *I* decided to make. My techniques, on my plate, food inspired by me, and a family who would be delighted about it.

Completely wrong.

My clients like humble, good food. Some nights that means chicken fingers and fries — not an elevated version with a clever sauce, just chicken fingers and fries, because that is what the kids want.

That was a real ego buster, and I do not want to pretend otherwise. There is a period where you feel like the skills you spent years building are going unused, and it stings.

What got me through it was a shift I actually believe now: it is never about what I want. It is always about what the client wants. And once I stopped grieving the plate I was not making, I got a lot better at the job.

There is also this, which took me longer to understand. My internship at Alo taught me exactly how much labor sits underneath a plate that somebody pays a great deal of money for, and I respect that work enormously. But fine dining never gave me a way to *connect* to food. It is not the kind of food you connect to the way you connect to a meal your grandmother cooked on a Sunday. That food is recognizable. It is what a family comes together for. It is cooked with love and people bond over it.

That is the food I get to make now. The oxtail hitting the table again. Watching a family share a meal and actually enjoy each other.

I did not choose that as a consolation prize. I chose it because it is the thing I wanted the whole time.

## The 90/10 split

Consistency versus creativity runs about 90/10 for me.

Ninety percent of the time, my clients want food they have already eaten, because it is food they already know they like. If that is what they want, that is what I make.

The ten percent is where I get flare — something new, fun, creative, that they get to try. Some houses sit closer to 60/40. It genuinely depends on the person, and you will learn your family's ratio within a couple of months.

The dish I have made more times than I can count is Jamaican oxtail and chicken. They love it. They request it almost every single week.

There is nothing on that plate that would impress another chef. It is just correct, every time.

**So here is how to actually build a house menu.** Start with what they already request. Write those dishes down — that list is your ninety percent, and it is more valuable than any concept you could invent. The ten percent is one new thing, introduced when they have room for it.

You do not need a new tasting menu every Tuesday. Trying to force one is one of the most common mistakes I see restaurant chefs make when they transition, and clients read it as the chef performing for himself rather than feeding them.

## You are building a week, not a dish

A household day for me looks like this. Breakfast at 10. Lunch at 1:30. Snacks and baked goods in between. Kids' dinner in the early evening. Adults eat at 8.

Same mouths, same times, every day. Which means provisioning is far lighter than events, because you can actually predict them. That predictability is the gift of household work — use it. Build a rotation. Learn what runs out on a Thursday.

Events are the exact opposite. Every private dinner is a different menu. I plan days ahead, shop the day before, and show up about five hours early. That is where the creative side of my brain gets air, and it is a big part of why I keep Thursday through Saturday open for them.

If you need the tasting-menu high, this is where you put it. Not into a Tuesday night in a house of five.

## Dietary needs are the menu

Dietary complexity is only increasing, and it will keep increasing as people become more health-conscious and better informed. Five years ago this was simpler — fewer restrictions, less awareness, less action taken around eating well.

Cooking one meal that serves everyone's needs is genuinely difficult. But as a private chef, that difficulty is not an obstacle to the job. It is the job. You have to know how.

And the practical version is unglamorous. If two people in a house of five cannot eat the same plate, you are building two plates. That is your menu. Not a concept, not a theme — the actual people sitting at that table and what their bodies can handle.

## Time is part of the menu

My biggest mistake in year one was time management, and it shows up directly in menu building.

In a restaurant, other chefs hold you up. There is a prep cook, a dish pit, someone who ordered the produce. As a private chef, the cooking, the cleaning, the menu planning, and the grocery run all sit inside one day, on you. I had no sense of how much time each task deserved or how to fit them together, and I spent that first year constantly behind.

So: write the menu, then write the minutes. Grocery. Prep. Cook. Clean.

If a menu only works with a brigade behind you, it is the wrong menu for a house of four. That is not a compromise, it is just arithmetic.

And build in slack, because the clock will move. Dinner is set for 6pm and then it is not. Four extra people, one hour out. You can cook for them, but dinner shifts and the cost goes up, and you say so out loud. A house menu that only works on a perfect clock will fail you, probably on a night when it matters.

## Staying sharp without boring them, or yourself

Here is the thing nobody warns you about. You work alone in this job, and your learning can come to a complete halt if you do not deliberately keep it going. There is no chef across the pass showing you something new. Two years can pass and your technique is exactly where it was.

I stay on YouTube. I stage in restaurants when I can, specifically to keep the technical side alive.

Then I use that on my ten percent. What I do *not* do is dump a stage dish onto kids who asked for chicken fingers, because that is cooking for myself in someone else's house.

The win in this job is not applause. The win is the oxtail hitting the table again, the family eating together, and nobody thinking about me at all.
