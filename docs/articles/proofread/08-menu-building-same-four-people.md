# Menu Building: Cooking for the Same Four People, 300 Nights a Year

Jordan

My households are usually 4 to 7 people. I am in one full-time house Monday to Wednesday. Thursday to Saturday I open for events. The house is the same family, over and over. Events are a new menu each time.

I thought private cheffing meant I would cook the food I decided to make. Techniques I wanted on the plate. Food that was inspired by me. That was wrong. Clients like humble good food. Sometimes I am cooking chicken fingers and fries. That was a big ego buster.

It is not what you want to cook. It is what they want to eat.

## The split

Consistency versus creativity is about a 90 / 10 split for me. Most of the time they want the thing they already eat. If that is what they want, that is what you make. The 10 percent is where you get a flare. Something new, fun, creative. Some houses sit closer to 60 / 40. Depends on the person.

One dish I have made a bunch of times is Jamaican oxtail and chicken. They love it. They ask for it almost every week.

If you are building a house menu, start with what they already request. Write those down. Those are the 90 percent. The 10 percent is one new thing when they have room for it. You do not need a new tasting menu every Tuesday.

## The week, not the dish

A household day for me is breakfast at 10, lunch at 1:30, snacks and baked goods in between, kids' dinner, adults at 8. You are feeding the same mouths at those times. Provisioning is lighter than events because you can predict them.

Events are the opposite. Each private dinner is different. You plan the menu days ahead, shop the day before, show up five hours early. That is where the creative brain gets more air.

Dietary needs stack more than they did five years ago. People are more health-conscious. Cooking one meal that covers everyone is hard. You still have to know how. If two people in a house of five cannot eat the same plate, you are building two plates. That is the menu. Not a concept. The people at the table.

## Time is part of the menu

Year one I got time management wrong. In a restaurant other chefs hold you up. In a house you cook, you clean, you plan the menu, you shop. All of that sits in one day. I did not know how much time each task deserved.

Write the menu, then write the minutes. Grocery. Prep. Cook. Clean. If the menu only works with a brigade behind you, it is the wrong menu for a house of four.

Dinner can be set for 6pm and then it is not. Four more people, one hour out. You can cook for them. Dinner moves. Cost goes up. A house menu that only works on a perfect clock will fail.

## Stay sharp without boring them, or yourself

You work alone. Learning can halt if you stop pushing. I tell chefs to stay on YouTube and to stage in restaurants when they can, so the technical side does not die. Use that on the 10 percent. Do not dump a stage dish onto kids who asked for chicken fingers.

Alo taught me what labor sits under a fancy plate. I respect that. I do not connect to that food the way I connect to a Sunday meal at grandma's. The house wants the Sunday meal most nights. That is why I am in houses.

If you need the tasting-menu high, take a stage on a night off. In the house, the win is the oxtail hitting the table again and nobody thinking about you.
